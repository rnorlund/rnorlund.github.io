What One T1 Scan Reveals: The Brain Health Network One-Pager

One of those projects that started as a figure-generation convenience and quietly became a product. Brain Health Network (BHN) takes a single T1-weighted MRI (optionally paired with a FLAIR) and emits a one-page clinical-style readout that a radiologist could glance at in ten seconds and understand everything the sequence is capable of telling them. The whole pipeline runs in a browser tab on commodity CPU. No upload. No account. No PACS integration.

The page above is a real BHN output. From a single T1 it extracts a 104-region parcellation, computes a regional brain-age estimate with a mean absolute error of about seven years, shows that estimate against a normative cohort distribution, breaks the brain into cortical lobe volumes, computes the normative percentile for every macro-structure, and ranks the regions pushing the age estimate up versus down. Then it runs the pathology screens that T1 (and when present, FLAIR) can honestly support: chronic infarct via the ARC MeshNet, white-matter hyperintensities via a WMH MeshNet, and acute-infarct and tumor flagged as "NOT ASSESSED" with a note on which sequences would be needed. The report ends with abnormality flags, so a reader can navigate from headlines to detail.

Two design choices I keep defending. First, contrast-agnostic parcellation: the models were trained to produce the same label map regardless of T1 vendor, scanner, or contrast quirks. That matters because the use case is "a scan arrives from somewhere else," not "a scan comes out of our own protocol." Second, honest about what it cannot do: every panel that would need a sequence the user did not upload says so, in plain language, with the reason. "Acute infarct NOT ASSESSED, requires DWI/ADC" is more useful to a clinician than a quiet shrug.

BHN is the serving layer underneath several things we have shipped this year: the brain-mets segmentation BraTS entry (where it provides the anatomy and tissue context around the tumor); a planned longitudinal viewer for ABC and C-STAR cohort subjects; and an early version of the open-neuroimaging tooling for the C-STAR team's chart reviews. The research version is private; a wider release depends on the usual DUA sign-offs. If you work in neuroimaging and want a demo, email me.

navier-stokes-needle: Reverse-Engineering a Piece of Procedural Art

I saw a loop of someone's procedural fluid-dynamics art on a feed and could not stop thinking about it. The piece is a thin vertical needle piercing a flat, slightly rippled disk, with little wavy rings fluttering around the intersection and a gentle color sweep from indigo to teal across the whole shape. The motion is physical, not keyframed, which means it has to be solving something real. I wanted to know what.

Side-quest weekend: build the minimal physics simulation that reproduces it. The disk is a two-dimensional velocity field on a cylindrical patch, perturbed at the center by the needle's displacement. The rings are what you get from a shallow-water or Navier-Stokes solver when you poke the fluid vertically and let the surface relax. The gentle sheen is just a surface-normal-dot-view shader on the velocity magnitude. One hundred lines of JavaScript, canvas, and a WebGL fragment program. Everything runs in the browser.

Image on the left is a single frame. The full version animates, with the needle slowly rotating and the rings running around it on their own timescale. The rotation and ring frequencies are tunable; different values produce visibly different "species" of ring (floppy and lazy versus crisp and quick). I ended up writing a small tuner site so you can swipe through the parameter space and find your own.

Why this exists on a research blog: the same shallow-water solver with slightly different boundary conditions is used in a lot of serious physics-informed-ML work right now. Reproducing a piece of generative art on top of it was a sideways way to actually understand the solver by instrumenting it until it rendered something I recognized. Side projects as cross-training again. The tuner site is live at orbit-tuner-site, deploy pending; code lands on GitHub once I clean it up.

Card Quilt, Now Rendered Server-Side as a Poster

The Card Quilt widget was always a browser toy. Pick a grid, pick some cards, animate a stitch between them. Fun to play with, slow to load as a static preview because the full interactive version needs to pull the whole image pack and warm up the WebGL animation before anything shows up. On the personal site that was fine; on a landing page it was not.

The fix was a two-stage render. The site now ships with a pre-rendered poster of each widget (quilt, Fibonacci spiral, geode, cortex, mosaic, plus a shading library of fifteen or so looks) baked out at build time by a small Python + Playwright pipeline. The poster lives at the top of the fold and loads instantly. If the visitor lingers, the interactive version swaps in behind it on the next animation frame and takes over. If they scroll past, the poster was the whole experience and there was no wasted compute.

Image above is the real poster for the Card Quilt: four cards from one of my binders (Abduction, Abstruse Interference, Abbot of the Sacred Meeple, a signed Erica Will illustration) stitched together with the gold chain pattern that marks my quilt variant, with the live settings panel visible on the right so you can see what the interactive version offers. The whole thing is a 400 KB JPEG at this point, which is less than one of the card art textures used to be.

Smaller point, bigger idea: a lot of interactive sites can be served as posters-plus-upgrades. You pay zero load time for the common case, you get the full interactive experience for the engaged case, and the search engines index what the posters show. I keep coming back to this pattern.

Journal Picker: One Page, Honest Prices, No Publisher Bias

Every author has had this moment: a paper is done, it is time to pick a journal, and the publisher-owned finders are useless. Elsevier's tool will never suggest a Wiley journal. Wiley's finder will never recommend a Frontiers one. Standalone similarity-matchers can tell you the abstract looks like something Brain Communications publishes, but not what the article processing charge is, how long peer review will take, or whether your institution has already paid.

So I built Journal Picker. It is a single page. You paste a title and an abstract. It searches the biomedical literature for similar papers, ranks the journals that actually publish them, and shows the four things authors actually need to decide:

  • Match score. Based on similar-papers-they-published, not abstract-shape alone. The strongest signal in the stack is the overlap between a journal's recent publications and the reference list you paste in. Journals you cite more are journals your work is more likely to belong in.
  • Real article processing charges. Pulled live from DOAJ, not scraped from marketing pages. "Free" and "open access" are modelled as different things, because they are.
  • Honest review turnaround. Median submission-to-acceptance in days, computed from PubMed's publisher-deposited dates. 3,700+ journals have real distributions; the rest get flagged as unreliable rather than estimated.
  • Institutional coverage. Upload your university's open-access agreement list and the ranked results show which journals your library already pays for.

No account. No API key. No publisher sponsor. 32,565 journals indexed across 16 fields. Deliberately missing: fabricated metrics (no invented acceptance rates, no scraped impact factors, no "editor friendliness" scores). The tool is only as honest as its data lets it be.

Live at journal-picker.netlify.app. The repo is private while I polish the licensing and the catalog build; a public release follows when both are ready.

Soda City Crush: The Smallest Possible Dating App

Weekend side-project I keep thinking about more than I expected to. Soda City Crush is a front-end prototype of a local, slow, genuinely small dating app for Columbia, SC. Zero backend. Zero framework. Zero npm install. Every account, profile, assessment answer, and message lives in the visitor's own browser via localStorage. The entire "server" is a static-file host that happens to speak one POST endpoint for the owner's own content edits.

The design question that interests me is what you have to subtract before online dating becomes bearable again. The default stack (push notifications, endless swipe queue, algorithmic match engine, premium subscription, accounts tied to real identity before you have decided whether you want to be here) all serves the platform. None of it is for the user. Soda City Crush removes every one of those by construction. There is no notification engine because there is no server. There is no infinite feed because the pool is one Southern-small-city of actual people. There is no premium tier because there is no revenue model. The ceiling on growth is intentional.

Three features worth noting. First, the six-word profile: your bio is literally six words, no more. Second, a compatibility assessment that runs locally against the other person's locally-stored profile vector when you tap their card, so neither party's profile ever leaves their machine; the match score is a client-side computation. Third, a message-first UX: there is no "like" verb, you either message someone a question or you do not, and the question has to be answerable in a reply.

None of this is live. It is a prototype on my laptop with brand copy drafted, three CSS themes, and the storage-migration layer written. Whether it ever ships depends on whether I can convince myself that online dating in 2026 can be made both smaller and better at the same time, and whether a Columbia-only dating app with no notifications is a product people actually want. Jury out.

In the Form of a Question: A Jeopardy-Shaped Essay About My Father

I drafted a long-form essay this fall about my father and the word that would not come. He plays Jeopardy almost every night. He has for forty years. He is extraordinary at it, in the specific way lifelong Jeopardy watchers are extraordinary: fast on the categories he loves (geography, history, Broadway), slow on the ones he does not, aware of his own ranges. For the last two years he has been slower. For the last six months he has been slower in a way that is no longer compatible with any explanation except the one his family does not want to say out loud.

The essay is called "In the Form of a Question." It is about one phone call. He was trying to describe his wireless printer, which had stopped talking to his computer, to anyone who would listen. The remedy was to turn it off and back on, which was out of his reach for reasons that are nobody's fault. He is a man who had the printer manual memorized a decade ago. The call lasted twenty minutes and I spent most of it listening to him search for the word reboot.

The essay is structured like a game of Jeopardy. Five acts, each a category, each ending with a clue that only makes sense once you have the answer. I am submitting it to the New Yorker. If they pass, I will keep sending it until a magazine takes it. I am not posting the text here because it is in review, but I wanted to note the project exists because this blog has mostly been software and brain scans this year, and this is the thing I actually spent August and September thinking about.

Flagging this one explicitly: it is personal and about my dad, not me, but it is still family. Skip if you want to keep the blog technical.
